## Q1. What are typical TCC enable criteria, and how does a failed input prevent lock-up or cause it? (A.18, A.22)

### Takeaway
The module (PCM/TCM) applies the TCC only when fluid and engine are warm, speed and gear are high enough, throttle is steady and not closed or at WOT, and the brake is off. It uses the brake switch, TPS, ECT/TFT, and VSS/OSS/ISS to decide. A failed input usually **prevents** lock-up. A brake switch reading "applied", a TPS dead spot, or a cold-reading temperature sensor all do this. Some failures do the opposite. A lost OSS/VSS signal can leave the TCC applied as the car stops, and an overheat reading (real or false) can make the module lock up **earlier and harder** to shed heat. Always check the inputs and the "TCC enable" PID before condemning the solenoid or converter.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (principle), OEM example values: Lock-up is cancelled when the brake light switch comes on, when the TPS idle contacts close (throttle fully closed), or when coolant temperature is below 70 °C (158 °F). Another calibration is quoted as holding lock-up off until coolant reaches as high as 120 °F. — search summary drawing on [MOTOR, Torque Converter Lock-up (May 1999)](https://www.motor.com/magazine-summary/torque-converter-lock-may-1999/) and [LiveAbout, Diagnosing GM Converter Lock-Up Problems](https://www.liveabout.com/diagnosing-gm-converter-lock-up-problems-262653). Which source gave which number is unclear. The temperature thresholds vary by calibration (SI).
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: The brake pedal position (BPP) switch tells the PCM the brakes are applied, and the TCC disengages. The PCM uses TFT to inhibit TCC during warm-up. — same search summary ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/diagnosing-gm-converter-lock-up-problems-262653); [MOTOR 1999](https://www.motor.com/magazine-summary/torque-converter-lock-may-1999/))
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM-hobbyist level: Loss of TPS signal or WOT locks out TCC. So do a pressed brake switch, the current gear, and TFT. On the quoted (older GM) calibration, VSS must be above about 45 mph for full lock-up. — [GMForum, No TCC lockup](https://www.gmforum.com/2000-2005-90/no-tcc-lockup-274062/) / [GMTNation, TCC locks & unlocks frequently](https://gmtnation.com/forums/threads/tcc-locks-unlocks-frequently.13844/) (forum excerpts; the 45 mph figure is one calibration only; SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (DTCs generic): P0719 = torque converter/brake switch "B" circuit low. P0724 = circuit high. The switch is part of the system that cancels lock-up and cruise control. Symptoms include a TCC that **does not lock up** or a lock-up that **will not disengage**. Common causes are the brake lamp switch, a blown lamp fuse, or wiring. "The most common misdiagnosis is to blame the lock-up solenoid or the wiring, rather than to check the stop lamp switch, the lighting controller, or the brake lamp connectors." — [YourMechanic, P0719](https://www.yourmechanic.com/article/p0719-obd-ii-trouble-code-torque-converter-brake-switch-b-circuit-low-by-yourmechanic); [RepairPal, P0724](https://repairpal.com/obd-ii-code-p0724-brake-switch-b-circuit-high); [troublecodes.net P0719](https://www.troublecodes.net/pcodes/p0719/) (consumer/code sites; consistent with one another)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (principle): An intermittent TPS dead spot can cause TCC cycling on and off without setting a code. At constant speed, the brake circuit and TPS are the most likely culprits for intermittent TCC cycling. — [GMTNation](https://gmtnation.com/forums/threads/tcc-locks-unlocks-frequently.13844/) / [MonteCarloSS forum](https://www.montecarloss.com/community/ubbthreads.php?ubb=printthread&Board=21&main=43435&type=thread) (forum excerpts; low authority but mechanically sound)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: With no OSS signal (P0722), the TCC may not lock up. The engine may also stall when braking because the computer fails to disengage the TCC. The transmission may go into a failsafe/limp gear. — [RepairPal, P0722](https://repairpal.com/obd-ii-code-p0722); [OBD-Codes, P0722](https://www.obd-codes.com/p0722) (excerpts; consumer level; the exact failsafe behavior is SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (2007 GM Mode 6/OBD document): If ECT DTCs P0117/P0118 are set, or ECT is 125 °C (257 °F) or more, the **default TFT** equals 135 °C (275 °F). If IAT DTCs are set or IAT is not available, the default TFT equals 90 °C (194 °F). — [GM, 07 GRP11 All Transmissions (gsitlc.ext.gm.com)](https://gsitlc.ext.gm.com/gmspo/mode6/pdf/2007/07%20GRP11_All%20Transmissions.pdf) (excerpt; GM 2007 calibration; the default logic is model-specific, so check SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (principle): When fluid is too hot, the module commands lock-up to shed heat, using a separate "hot" lock-up schedule (a patent example gives 120 °C as the switch point). Depending on which sensor fails, the transmission may see an **early full apply** of the TCC. One source says an overheat signal makes the computer fully apply the TCC slightly above about 1,200 rpm. — search summary drawing on [US Patent 5050717, Temperature responsive lock-up control](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5050717) and [VehicleServicePros, Can you feel the heat?](https://www.vehicleservicepros.com/service-repair/underhood/article/21186094/can-you-feel-the-heat) (excerpts; numbers are examples only; SI)

### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: A failed input can go wrong in either direction, which is exam-relevant:
  - **Prevents lock-up:** a brake switch stuck "applied", an open or misadjusted brake switch (on designs where open means applied), TPS at 0% or WOT or erratic, ECT/TFT reading cold, a missing speed signal (no speed means the module won't enable), or a gear-ratio or other transmission DTC that puts it in failsafe.
  - **Causes unwanted or early lock-up:** a TFT or ECT reading falsely hot (hot-mode schedule, earlier full apply), or a lost OSS/VSS signal while the TCC is already applied (the module can't see the car slowing, so it may not release before stop).
  - **Causes cycling:** an intermittent brake switch (bouncing contacts, a misadjusted switch, a brake-lamp circuit fault), a TPS dead spot, or a VSS signal that drops out.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: For a strong electrical tech, the fast check is the scan tool. Look at the brake switch PID (toggle the pedal), TPS sweep, ECT/TFT plausibility (compare cold-soak readings with each other and with ambient), and speed-sensor agreement. Do this **before** any hydraulic or converter test. That is an A.22 "engine/input vs. transmission" discrimination.
- INFERRED / GM: On many GM applications the TCC brake input shares the stop-lamp circuit (hence "lamp fuse" as a cause). Whether a given vehicle uses a separate TCC brake switch contact, a BPP sensor, or a CAN message is SI.

### Gaps
- OPEN: I couldn't reach a primary textbook (Halderman or Today's Technician) list of enable criteria, and no GM SI enable table for a current unit (6L80/8L90/10L). Actual thresholds (TFT, ECT, minimum gear, speed, throttle window) are SI.
- OPEN: The switch sense convention (normally open vs. normally closed TCC brake contact) varies by year and model and was not verified.

## Q2. How do two-pass and three-pass converters apply and release, and how do on/off, PWM, and controlled-slip (EC3) strategies differ? (A.13, A.18)

### Takeaway
In a **two-pass** converter, apply and release share the converter's own charge flow. Release oil enters between the cover and the piston and pushes the piston off the cover. To apply, the release passage is exhausted and converter charge (apply) pressure pushes the piston onto the cover. A **three-pass** converter adds a dedicated third passage just for piston apply and release, so converter charge and cooling flow continue during lock-up (better heat control). The control strategy developed from **on/off** solenoids (hard apply, fully released or fully locked) to **PWM** solenoids (a controlled apply rate) to **controlled or continuous slip** (GM EC3 from about 1996–98). Controlled slip holds a small, deliberate slip for NVH isolation and needs special friction material and fluid. Modern units use a variable-force or PC solenoid and command a slip target.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: In a conventional two-path converter, there is an "apply" passage and a "release" passage, named for the TCC state. For release, fluid enters through the tip of the input shaft and flows between the converter cover and the TCC piston, pushing the piston away from the cover. — [Gears, Exploring the Function and Construction of Two- and Three-Path Torque Converters](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/exploring-the-function-and-construction-of-two-and-three-path-torque-converters/) (excerpt)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL with OEM examples: In a three-passage converter, two passages carry ATF flow through the converter and for clutch cooling, and the third is used independently to lock and unlock the clutch. Some units (Ford AX4N, CD4E, and some imports) use this design. The third circuit "virtually eliminated the overheating condition of the two-path." Ford calls the third circuit Converter Bypass (CBY). — [Gears, Two- and Three-Path](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/exploring-the-function-and-construction-of-two-and-three-path-torque-converters/); [Sonnax, Ford Bypass Converter Clutch Circuits](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/203-ford-bypass-converter-clutch-circuits); [x-engineer, Torque converter clutch](https://x-engineer.org/torque-converter-clutch/) (excerpts)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (two-pass physics): "If the converter is full, lockup will begin to apply as soon as the release oil is opened to exhaust, because normal working pressures within the converter are always trying to push the TCC piston toward the cover." — [Sonnax, Troubleshooting Lockup Issues](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/97-troubleshooting-lockup-issues) (also at [Transmission Digest](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/troubleshooting-lockup-issues/)) (excerpt)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (700-R4/4L60 to 4L60-E): Before the mid-1990s change from the 700-R4/4L60 on-off converter circuit to the 4L60-E PWM-controlled circuit, TCC apply and release were simply on or off. In the 4L60-E, the PCM energizes the TCC solenoid, the converter clutch valve strokes, release fluid exhausts, and the apply circuit opens. A second (PWM) solenoid controls a regulated apply valve, and its duty cycle sets the rate at which the TCC applies and releases. — [Sonnax, GM TCC Circuits](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/137-gm-tcc-circuits) (excerpt)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (older GM): "When grounded (energized) by the PCM, the TCC solenoid valve stops converter signal oil from exhausting. When the PCM no longer provides a ground path, the TCC solenoid valve de-energizes and apply fluid exhausts, releasing the TCC." — excerpt via [autocodes P0742 Chevrolet](https://www.autocodes.com/p0742_chevrolet.html) / [ls2.com GM DTC P0742 page](https://www.ls2.com/boggs/a4/DTC%20P0742%205.7.htm) (appears to reproduce GM SI text; attribution between the two is uncertain)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM: EC3 (Electronically Controlled Capacity Clutch) was introduced in 1997 on W-body 3.4L cars and used across all models in 1998. It is "a combination of software and hardware that allows the TCC to slip constantly within a predetermined RPM window." The quoted example of EC3 duty-cycle behavior: the PWM duty drops to 0%, rises to about 25%, then ramps to about 40%, holding a constant 20–40 rpm slip. Full lock is commanded only under high torque or high vehicle speed; otherwise it always slips. The slip-apply strategy can start as early as 2nd gear. — [VehicleServicePros, 4L60-Evolution](https://www.vehicleservicepros.com/service-repair/underhood/article/21182663/4l60-evolution); [Sonnax, A Slippery Slope: woven carbon friction](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/254-a-slippery-slope-the-what-and-why-of-woven-carbon-friction-material); [TechShop, ACDelco EC3](https://www.techshopmag.com/acdelco-s-electronically-controlled-capacity-clutch-ec3-makes-troublesome-transmission-conditions-concerns-of-the-past/) (excerpts; duty/slip numbers are one article's example; SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM plus aftermarket: EC3 uses a **woven carbon** lining developed by GM with AvCarb, instead of paper, so it survives constant slip. The aftermarket long struggled to match it, and Sonnax began offering authentic OE woven carbon to rebuilders in late 2015. Woven carbon (or equivalent) must be used in all EC3 applications. — [Sonnax, A Slippery Slope](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/254-a-slippery-slope-the-what-and-why-of-woven-carbon-friction-material); [Sonnax, Living with EC3: The Identification Process (PDF mirror)](https://www.automaticchoice.com/uploads/producten/pdfs/Catalogue/Sonnax/EC3_Identification_Sonnax.pdf) (excerpts)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (modern strategy): Current vehicles "anticipate and manage torque converter clutch application by allowing for a range of slippage to full apply," using engineering data to avoid predictable NVH conditions while commanding as little slip as possible. The article lists shudders, sluggish feel, busy shifting, and slipping during shifts as problems with these strategies, and recommends checking for the latest PCM/TCM programming as part of diagnosis. — [Gears, Lock Up Madness! GM and Ford TCC Control](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/lock-up-madness-gm-and-ford-torque-converter-clutch-control/) (excerpt)

### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: In a two-pass design, **anything that reduces release flow** (a low pump output or PR valve out of balance, a worn bushing letting release oil cross, a restricted feed) lets the piston drag on the cover. That produces **unwanted partial apply**: a stuck-on feel, stalling, or harsh or early lock-up. It happens even with a good solenoid. See the Sonnax flow findings in Q6.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: Which strategy the vehicle uses changes what "normal" looks like on the scan tool. On/off gives a step change in slip and rpm. PWM gives a ramp. Controlled slip shows a small, **non-zero** steady slip that is normal. Read slip data against the strategy, not against a "must be 0 rpm" rule.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: A converter rebuilt with the wrong friction material for a controlled-slip application is a known source of shudder after replacement. This follows from the woven-carbon requirement above. Verify the converter part number and application for any post-repair shudder.

### Gaps
- OPEN: No public excerpt described the apply/release circuit of a current GM unit (6L80, 8L90, 10L80, 9T65) in detail. These use a TCC pressure-control solenoid feeding a regulator valve; the circuit detail is SI.
- OPEN: Whether a given GM unit uses a two-pass or three-pass converter wasn't verified here (SI or rebuilder catalogs).

## Q3. How are TCC slip PIDs read, and how are the road test, brake-tap test, scan-tool command, and solenoid circuit tests done? (A.18, A.13)

### Takeaway
The core data is **engine rpm vs. turbine/input speed = TCC slip speed**, read alongside **TCC commanded state/desired slip** and **TCC solenoid duty cycle or commanded pressure**. With the TCC applied and steady, actual slip should follow desired slip. That means near zero for full lock, or a small steady value for controlled slip. **Large, rising, or oscillating slip while commanded on is the fault picture.** The brake-tap test (hold steady cruise, touch the brake without slowing, watch rpm rise then fall) is a quick functional check of release and re-apply, and of the brake input. Scan-tool bidirectional control (GM GDS2 "TCC enable/disable/lock") isolates the TCC as the cause of a symptom. Solenoid circuit tests are standard: resistance, supply, commanded ground or duty, and module-detected electrical DTCs. Specs come from SI.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (DTC logic, generic example): P0741 (TCC circuit performance/stuck off) is described as setting when the PCM sees **more than a 200 rpm** difference between engine/converter speed and input shaft speed. Another source says sustained slip "commonly in the 100–200 RPM range" sets it. "If the Lockup System is functioning correctly, the Slip Speed value should never be above 50 RPM." — search summary drawing on [RepairPal, P0741](https://repairpal.com/obd-ii-code-p0741); [Edmunds, P0741](https://www.edmunds.com/obd-dtc/p0741.html); [Sun Transmissions, P0741](https://suntransmissions.com/blogs/news/p0741-code-how-to-diagnose-the-torque-converter-clutch-and-find-the-right-fix) (consumer/vendor excerpts; the thresholds conflict between sources and are calibration-specific; needs SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM 6L80, informal: TCC slip should read near zero, but positive and negative values that change with load are normal, and allowances vary before a code sets ("normally no more than 75 rpm"). On 6L80, 0–50 rpm is typical "as long as you don't see it cycling in a pattern or shuddering. These transmissions don't sit at 0 ever, but seeing 100 rpm with steady throttle is likely a problem." Desired slip varies with gear and conditions. At low throttle and low torque they tend never to lock completely, especially with DOD (cylinder deactivation) enabled. — [diag.net, Anyone have TCC slip data for 6L80](https://diag.net/msg/m2dy33lhdtt1k01vmcjc8ybpqq); [HP Tuners forum, 6L80 TCC slip fluctuations](https://forum.hptuners.com/showthread.php?100965-6L80-TCC-Slip-fluctuations%2Fpage2=&p=724584#post724584) (technician/tuner forum excerpts; illustrative only, not spec; attribution between the two is uncertain)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: PIDs to watch are TCC enable, engine rpm, TCC slip speed, and TCC solenoid duty cycle. A PWM TCC solenoid gives more diagnostic information and helps catch intermittent or borderline problems. Capture a movie of the transmission data at the speed the symptom occurs, focusing on **TCC commanded slip vs. TCC actual slip**. — [MOTOR, Using a Scan Tool for Transmission Diagnosis](https://www.motor.com/magazine-summary/using-a-scan-tool-for-transmission-diagnosis/) (excerpt; the "movie" wording may come from a blended Gears Shudder Diagnosis excerpt, attribution uncertain)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (quick road check, informal sources): While cruising around 50 mph with throttle held steady, tap the brake pedal lightly. RPM should rise as the converter unlocks with the brake-light signal, then fall when it relocks. The quoted rise varies: "100–200", "200–300", and "about 400" rpm in different sources. — search summary drawing on [LiveAbout, Diagnosing GM Converter Lock-Up](https://www.liveabout.com/diagnosing-gm-converter-lock-up-problems-262653); [Roadrunner Converters, How to Test a Torque Converter](https://www.roadrunnerconverters.com/testing-diagnosing-torque-converters/); [HowStuffWorks](https://auto.howstuffworks.com/under-the-hood/vehicle-maintenance/how-to-test-torque-converter.htm) (consumer/rebuilder excerpts; the rpm rise depends on gear, load, and converter design, so there is **no spec**)

Resistance also changes with temperature.)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (older GM P0742 case): To tell an internal fault from an external one on a TCC stuck-on complaint, unplug the main transmission connector (20-way on the cited unit). ### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: How to read slip:
  - **Commanded off:** slip = normal converter slip. It is high at low speed and under load, and shrinks at cruise.
  - **Commanded on, full lock:** slip settles near zero, with small plus/minus oscillation that is normal.
  - **Commanded on but slip stays high or keeps rising:** the TCC isn't holding (stuck off, low apply pressure, a leak, or worn lining). This is the P0741 picture.
  - **Slip near zero with the TCC commanded off, or at idle/stop:** stuck on (P0742).
  - **Slip oscillating in a rhythm under steady throttle while the TCC applies or controls slip:** shudder (see Q5).
  - **Negative slip** (engine slower than turbine) is normal on coast or decel.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: What each test proves:
  - The **brake-tap** test checks the brake input and the release/re-apply sequence.
  - A **scan-tool command** bypasses the enable logic, so it separates "the module won't enable it" (an input problem, Q1) from "the module commands it but it doesn't hold" (hydraulic or converter).
  - **Circuit tests** separate electrical from hydraulic. A good circuit plus a correct command, with the TCC still not holding, points to hydraulic or converter causes (Q6).
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: GM uses different PID names (e.g. "TCC Slip Speed", "TCC Desired Slip", "TCC PC Solenoid Commanded Pressure", "TCC Enable") across units and scan tools. Use SI and GDS2 for exact names.

## Q4. Symptom to cause: no lock-up, stuck on, cycling, and lock-up clunk (A.13, A.18, A.9)

### Takeaway
- **No lock-up / stuck off (P0741):** high cruise rpm, poor mpg, and fluid overheating. Causes, from inputs outward: enable inputs and module (brake switch, TPS, TFT/ECT, speed sensors, programming), then the solenoid or its circuit, then the valve body (sticking valve, worn TCC regulator/modulator/apply bores), then leakage (stator-support bushing, turbine-shaft seal), then the converter itself (worn lining, internal piston seal).
- **Stuck on (P0742):** stalls or nearly stalls coming to a stop, or dies when shifted into gear. Causes are a stuck or contaminated solenoid or valve, an electrical short keeping it energized, a lost speed signal, or low release flow dragging the piston.
- **Cycling:** intermittent inputs (brake switch, TPS, VSS) or a borderline hydraulic hold.
- **Clunk on lock-up:** a harsh or unmodulated apply (solenoid or apply-rate control, high pressure), or mechanical slack (loose converter bolts, cracked flexplate, bad mounts). The slack is revealed by the torque step at apply.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: With P0741, a key symptom is higher-than-normal engine rpm at highway speed because the converter can't lock at 1:1. Causes listed are the TCC solenoid (electrical open or short, mechanically stuck, or clogged by debris), a worn converter, internal transmission leaks, the valve body, the TCM/PCM, and TCC circuit wiring or connectors. Wrong-spec fluid can cause clutch slip and shudder. — [RepairPal, P0741](https://repairpal.com/obd-ii-code-p0741); [YourMechanic, P0741](https://www.yourmechanic.com/article/p0741-obd-ii-trouble-code-torque-converter-clutch-circuit-performance-or-stuck-off-by-jamahl-walker); [Sun Transmissions, P0741](https://suntransmissions.com/blogs/news/p0741-code-how-to-diagnose-the-torque-converter-clutch-and-find-the-right-fix) (consumer/vendor excerpts)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (4L60-E family) plus rebuilder: Worn TCC valve bores are a known cause of P0741. Wear at the TCC **modulator** valve bore reduces converter apply pressure and line pressure. Wear at the TCC **regulator** valve bore reduces TCC apply and converter charge pressure. In 4L60-E/4L65-E/4L70-E units, severe regulator-bore wear from the OE valve oscillating lets regulated apply pressure exhaust. ### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: Order the no-lock-up diagnosis from cheapest to most expensive, and from outside to inside:
  1. Check codes and software updates. Gears recommends checking for the latest PCM/TCM programming.
  2. Check the input PIDs and enable (Q1).
  3. Command the TCC and watch slip (Q3).
  4. Test the solenoid circuit.
  5. Check fluid level and condition.
  6. Do line/TCC pressure and cooler-flow tests (Q6).
  7. Inspect the valve body and bores.
  8. Inspect the pump cover/stator support bushing and turbine-shaft seals (these need removal).
  9. Only then condemn the converter.
  This matches the ASE "most likely / check first" style of reasoning.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: **Overheat plus P0741** is self-consistent: an unlocked converter at cruise generates heat, and valve-body bore wear also cuts converter charge and cooler flow. A worn TCC regulator bore can therefore cause *both* slip and overheating. Don't assume the overheating is a separate fault.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: A clunk exactly at lock-up with good scan data (a smooth commanded ramp) points more to driveline slack or mounts than to TCC control. A thud with a step-like command or no ramp points to control (solenoid, apply-rate valve, calibration).

## Q5. TCC shudder: causes, telling it apart from misfire, mounts, and converter balance (A.9, A.22, A.13)

### Takeaway
TCC shudder is **stick-slip** of the TCC friction at low slip speed. It feels like driving over rumble strips at light-throttle cruise, as the TCC applies or holds controlled slip. The main causes are degraded or wrong fluid (friction modifiers depleted, or moisture in the GM 8-speed case), worn or glazed lining, the wrong friction material for a controlled-slip strategy, and low clamping force from outside the converter.

GM's documented discriminator is a **three-mode test** (PIP5504, for GM 8-speed): true TCC shudder is present in **normal operation**, and **absent with the TCC disabled** and **absent with the TCC fully locked**. A *misfire or "chuggle"* follows engine firing frequency and shows up in misfire or cylinder-contribution data. GM's PIP5405A goes further: if commanding the TCC on eliminates the chuggle and slip is under about 30 rpm, follow the engine lean-misfire diagnosis.

Worn **engine/transmission mounts** (especially with cylinder deactivation) are a known mimic. **Converter imbalance** vibrates with engine rpm regardless of TCC state, and even in neutral.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: ATF friction modifiers let the clutch move smoothly from slipping to locked. Heat, oxidation, and clutch wear deplete them. At very low slip speeds, the lining can then grab and release rapidly (stick-slip), which is the shudder. — [Tomorrow's Technician, How Friction Modifiers Can Help Torque Converter Shudder](https://www.tomorrowstechnician.com/how-friction-modifiers-can-help-torque-converter-shudder/); [Lubegard, Science Friction](https://lubegard.com/science-friction/) (trade-magazine and additive-vendor excerpts. Lubegard sells an additive "fix", so treat it as a biased source for the remedy, but the stick-slip mechanism is consistent)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM (8L45/8L90): GM issued multiple bulletins on TCC shudder in the 8L45 and 8L90. TSB **18-NA-355** covers "torque converter clutch (TCC) shudder conditions on 8L45 and 8L90" (RPO M5N, M5T, M5U, M5X). It specifies a new fluid-exchange procedure and a new formula, **Mobil 1 Synthetic LV ATF HP** (GM P/N 19417577 per excerpt). The root cause cited was that the original fluid was **hygroscopic** (absorbed moisture), degrading friction-modifier chemistry. One source says LV ATF HP is required for all 8-speed repairs. Coverage is quoted as more than 4 million vehicles. — [Counterman, An Inexpensive Solution for GM TCC Shudder](https://www.counterman.com/an-inexpensive-solution-for-gm-torque-converter-clutch-shudder/); [GM Authority, 8-Speed GM Transmission Problems Fixed Via New TSB (Aug 2019)](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2019/08/tsb-seems-to-fix-8-speed-automatic-gm-transmission-problems/); [Sun Transmissions, GM 8L90 Shudder Fix & TSB 18-NA-355](https://suntransmissions.com/blogs/news/the-definitive-gm-8l90-transmission-shudder-fix-and-fluid-flush-bulletin) (trade and enthusiast-news excerpts; the TSB itself wasn't read. **Check the current bulletin revision, part number, and fluid quantity in GM SI.** The "20 quarts" and "4 million" figures are secondary-source claims.)

- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: To separate shudder from misfire, look at engine data: misfire counters, fuel-system data, cylinder contribution, and DTCs. ### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: A simple way to remember "commanded off" vs. "commanded locked":
  - **True TCC shudder** exists only while the clutch is *slipping under control or applying*. Commanding it fully **off** (open converter) or fully **locked** (no slip) both remove it. That is PIP5504 Modes B and C.
  - **Engine misfire** usually does **not** go away with the TCC locked. It is often *more* noticeable locked, because the fluid coupling no longer damps the pulses. It may soften with the TCC off, and it shows in misfire or cylinder-contribution data. Its frequency follows engine rpm.
  - **Mount or deactivation mimic** lines up with DOD transitions.
  - **Imbalance** follows engine rpm in any TCC state and even in neutral.
  - **Tire, driveshaft, or U-joint** vibration follows road speed.
  The assignment's "shudder stops when TCC is commanded off" is correct but incomplete. In GM's procedure it also stops when the TCC is fully *locked*.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: Fluid-first is the standard shudder remedy because the fluid is the cheapest and most common cause. The GM 8-speed case, a full exchange with a revised fluid, is the flagship example. If the lining is already damaged (glazed or burned), a fluid change may not cure it, and the converter needs replacement. This is sound reasoning, but no excerpt stated the order for GM; follow the TSB decision tree.
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: Shudder after a converter replacement should prompt checks for the wrong friction type (Q2), wrong fluid, and low clamping pressure from a valve-body or leakage problem the new converter can't fix (Sonnax clamping-force finding; Q6).

## Q6. Causes of TCC slip outside the converter (valve-body bores, stator-support bushing, turbine-shaft seals, cooler flow) vs. converter faults (A.13, A.22)

### Takeaway
Many "bad converter" complaints are really **apply-pressure loss before the oil reaches the piston**. The main examples are:
- worn TCC regulator, modulator, apply, or feed-limit valve bores;
- a worn **stator-support (pump cover) bushing**, which separates apply and release oil at the turbine shaft; on the cited unit, clearance of about .002" or more leaks enough to slip the TCC;
- worn or missing **turbine-shaft seals or O-ring**;
- a flow restriction, cooler flow problem, or PR valve imbalance.

Pressure and flow testing with the vehicle running (line or TCC pressure plus cooler flow, and cooler-loop bypass) separates these from true converter faults. True converter faults are lining wear, glazing, or delamination, a leaking piston seal, and cover deflection.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL principle, OEM example: TCC apply oil can leak past the front stator-support bushing. "A journal clearance of .002" or more will generate sufficient leakage to cause lockup clutch slippage." Symptoms: TCC slip codes, no lock-up, overheated fluid, no overdrive. GM 4L80-E/4L85-E units may show no lock-up or codes 68, 39, 1870, or P0741. — [Sonnax, Stator Support Bushing 34016-W](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/1982-stator-support-bushing) (excerpt; the .002" figure is Sonnax's for that application; SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL: Worn bushings (such as a stator-support bushing) separate apply and release oil. If one is excessively worn, apply oil can cross into the release circuit and cause TCC apply issues. — search summary drawing on [Sonnax, Troubleshooting Lockup Issues](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/97-troubleshooting-lockup-issues) and [Gears, Torque Converter Tech with Bob Warnke](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/torque-converter-tech-with-insights-from-bob-warnke/) (attribution uncertain)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / UNIVERSAL (forum-level, GM example): Two seals seal converter apply and release fluid. One is an O-ring on the end of the turbine shaft (the shaft sticking out of the front of the transmission), which should be replaced every time the converter is removed. — [LS1Tech, TCC slip after rebuild and new TC](https://ls1tech.com/forums/automatic-transmission/1835885-tcc-slip-after-rebuild-new-tc.html) / [batauto, 4l60e question](https://batauto.com/threads/4l60e-question.4848/) (forum excerpts; the seal layout is unit-specific; SI)
- DOCUMENTED (snippet) / GM OEM plus rebuilder (valve body): See Q4 for the TCC regulator, modulator, and feed-limit bore-wear findings ([Sonnax 56947J-15K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2232-tcc-modulator-valve-kit); [Sonnax 77754-04K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2458-tcc-regulator-isolator-valve-kit); [Sonnax 104520-11](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/1701-oversized-converter-feed-limit-valve)).

### Inferences
- INFERRED / UNIVERSAL: **Discriminator: converter fault vs. leakage outside the converter.**

  - **Pressure normal, circuit sealed, slip still high:** the converter itself (lining worn or glazed, piston seal, cover deflection).
  - **New converter didn't fix it:** suspect the bushing, seals, or valve-body bores. The Sonnax material and the tuner anecdote support this.

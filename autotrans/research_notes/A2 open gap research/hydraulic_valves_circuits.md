## 1. Pressure regulator valve and boost / reverse boost: what they do, why reverse pressure is higher, and what wear causes

### Takeaway
The pressure regulator (PR) valve balances pump pressure on one end against spring force plus boost-valve force on the other. It dumps the excess, first to the converter feed and then to the suction/exhaust. The boost valve adds force on the spring side from a torque signal (TV, modulator, or EPC/PC solenoid) and, in reverse, from reverse oil. That raises line pressure because reverse typically needs more holding force. Worn PR or boost bores give low or erratic line pressure, and the symptom depends on which part of the bore is worn.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: The reverse boost valve works with the PR valve to raise line pressure as engine torque increases. It also raises the operating range of line pressure in reverse. It is a spool valve in a sleeve, installed in the pump. Torque-signal pressure moves the boost valve against an isolator spring, which transfers that force to the PR valve and raises line pressure. — [US Patent 6619323 "Reverse boost valve assembly"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6619323); [US Patent 6776736 "Self-regulating reverse boost valve assembly"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6776736) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, mechanism] {A.14}: Reverse boost works mechanically. Reverse-circuit oil is routed to the spring end of the PR valve. The added force means the PR valve must see more pump pressure before it opens to dump, so regulated line pressure rises. — [Industrial Monitor Direct, "Reverse line pressure: why it's higher"](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/reverse-line-pressure-in-automatic-transmissions-why-its-higher) (snippet; **low-authority source**; the mechanism agrees with the patents above; its example psi values for the TH350 are NOT used here)
- OPEN [Universal]: Why reverse needs more pressure (the design reason). The snippets describe how the boost happens, not why. The common textbook explanation is that the reverse holding members (often a band or a clutch with a smaller piston area) carry high reaction torque from the reverse ratio. No snippet stated this. It is left as OPEN / INFERRED until textbook-verified.
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, A.17}: Wear on the balance end of the PR bore lets balance pressure cross-leak. The valve then needs higher line pressure to counter the PR spring. Wear between the line and exhaust passages cross-leaks line oil to exhaust and lowers overall line pressure. Symptoms differ with the worn area of the bore. Bore-wear symptoms usually get worse as the unit warms up and the fluid thins. — [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Main pressure regulator valve lineups"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/hydraulics-fundamentals-main-pressure-regulator-valve-lineups/); [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part I: Main PR Valve Line-Ups"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/674-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-i-main-pressure-regulator-valve-line-ups) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 4L60-E family] {A.14, A.17}: The reverse boost sleeve wears, allowing torque-signal and reverse oil to cross-leak or leak to sump. Complaints: poor reverse boost, delayed reverse, and low line pressure with poor upshift in manual low. EPC solenoid duty cycle and steel-sleeve vs. aluminum-bore thermal expansion accelerate the wear. PR bore wear near the small valve gives harsh or delayed reverse. Wear near the end plug gives soft shifts and low line rise. Overall symptoms: erratic line, soft upshifts, and low line rise in both forward and reverse. — Sonnax boost valve kit pages, e.g. [Sonnax Boost Valve Kit 77898E-3K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2481-boost-valve-kit); [Sonnax Boost Valve Kit 77898E-K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2484-boost-valve-kit) (snippet; the search summary blended several kit pages)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: A snippet described two further control points that refine line pressure. Secondary regulators exist beyond the main PR valve. Limit valves are "similar to regulator valves" but cap maximum pressure in a circuit. — [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part III: Secondary Regulators"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/678-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-iii-secondary-regulators); [Sonnax, "Part VI: Converter Limit Valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/844-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-vi-converter-limit-valves) (snippet)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: Reading a diagram: anything that adds force to the spring end of the PR valve (TV/modulator/EPC signal oil, reverse oil) raises line pressure. Anything that bleeds that signal away (a worn boost sleeve, a leaking signal circuit) lowers line pressure. On an EPC/PC-controlled GM unit, the PC solenoid output is the torque signal, so the same logic applies electronically.
- INFERRED {A.14}: "Low line rise in both forward and reverse" points to the common control (PR, boost, pump, EPC/signal). "Delayed or harsh reverse only" points to the reverse-specific boost path or reverse circuit (see section 10).

- OPEN: The textbook reason for higher reverse pressure (reaction torque / apply area) was not confirmed by any reachable source.

## 2. Manual valve and linkage: function, cross-leak, and misadjustment

### Takeaway
The manual valve is the driver-controlled gate that sends line pressure to the circuits for P, R, N, D and the manual ranges. A misadjusted linkage leaves it between detents. It then partially feeds, or leaks line pressure into, the wrong circuit. Results include creep in neutral, delayed engagements, dragging and burned clutches, and PRNDL/start problems. Manual-valve bores run looser than regulator bores, so they read lower on vacuum tests.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, B.6}: The manual valve connects hydraulic pressure to the clutches or brakes for Forward, the manual ranges, Reverse or Park, by way of shift and regulating valves. Manual valves can cause low-pressure and delayed-engagement complaints, which can lead to premature clutch failure. Manual valves normally have slightly more bore clearance, and so lower vacuum-test readings, than regulating valves. — [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Manual Valves"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/hydraulics-fundamentals-manual-valves/) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {B.6, A.14}: The manual shift valve is connected to the gear selector by the (often adjustable) shift cable. If it is adjusted wrong, the manual valve leaks line pressure into the wrong circuit. — [Free ASE Study Guides, "Automatic Transmission Manual Shift Valve Leak"](https://www.freeasestudyguides.com/transmission-manual-shift-valve.html) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {B.6}: Incorrect linkage adjustment can cause creeping in Neutral, premature clutch wear, delayed engagement in any gear, or no-start in Park/Neutral. Neutral drag overheats and damages the clutches. Excess linkage free-play can delay engagement and cause incomplete shifts. — [PartCatalog, "Automatic transmission shifter linkage problems"](https://www.partcatalog.com/blogs/transmission/automatic-transmission-shifter-linkage-problems-symptoms-fixes); [Engineer Fix, "How to adjust an automatic transmission shift cable"](https://engineerfix.com/how-to-adjust-an-automatic-transmission-shift-cable/) (snippet; both are commercial blogs of moderate authority, and they agree with the Free ASE Study Guides mechanism)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: This is a key diagram-reasoning point. A symptom that changes when you move the shifter slightly within a range (firm up or ease off) points at manual valve position or linkage before internal hardware. Check linkage and PRNDL/range-sensor alignment first.
- INFERRED [Universal]: On shift-by-wire units the manual valve may be positioned by an actuator, or eliminated. The "linkage" check then becomes a range-sensor/actuator check. [Needs SI]

### Gaps

## 3. Shift valves and relay/clutch-select valves: how solenoids (or legacy TV/governor) move them

### Takeaway
Shift valves are gatekeepers. Each routes line or clutch pressure to an apply circuit, or opens that circuit to exhaust. On electronic units, an on/off shift solenoid's signal oil strokes the valve against a spring, and the spring returns it when the solenoid switches off. On legacy hydraulic units, governor pressure (road speed) pushed one end of the shift valve. Throttle/TV pressure plus a spring (engine load) pushed the other end. The shift occurred when governor force won.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, A.9}: Shift valves connect line or clutch pressure to clutch apply and/or exhaust circuits. Modern shift valves are typically controlled by on/off shift solenoids and/or by clutch pressures from clutch pressure-control solenoids. When a shift solenoid turns ON it strokes a clutch select valve, and when OFF, spring force returns the valve. "Relay" valves work like shift valves ("receive and pass on"). — [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Shift & Relay Valves"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/tasc-force-tips-hydraulics-fundamentals-shift-relay-valves/); [Sonnax, "Part X: Shift & Relay Valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/930-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-x-shift-relay-valves) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, legacy] {A.14}: Governor and throttle valves feed opposite ends of the shift valve. Governor pressure tracks road speed and throttle pressure tracks engine load. As speed rises under light throttle, governor pressure overcomes throttle pressure plus the spring, and the valve moves to apply the next clutch or band, giving an upshift. — [BYU-Idaho AUTO 366 textbook, "Automatic Transmission Hydraulics"](https://books.byui.edu/auto_366_textbook/automatic_transmission_hydraulics) (snippet); corroborated by force-balance language in [US Patent 4306469](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4306469) and related shift-control patents (snippet)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal, legacy] {A.14}: Diagnostic logic from the force balance. Low governor pressure (stuck governor, leaking governor feed) gives late or no upshifts. High TV pressure gives late, firm shifts. Low TV pressure gives early, soft shifts (see section 9, which matches this for 700R4 TV cable misadjustment).
- INFERRED [Universal, electronic] {A.9, A.14}: A shift valve stuck in its stroked position mimics a solenoid stuck ON. A valve stuck at spring rest mimics a solenoid stuck OFF or open-circuited. A good solenoid circuit test with a wrong-gear symptom therefore points to the valve (varnish, debris, bore wear), not the solenoid.

## 4. TCC control: regulator, apply/control, enable and limit valves; apply vs release; two-pass vs three-pass converters

### Takeaway
In a two-pass converter, release oil enters through the turbine shaft and passes between the cover and the piston to hold the clutch off. TCC apply comes from reversing flow so apply oil pushes the piston onto the cover. The clutch friction itself separates the apply and release oil. A three-pass converter adds a dedicated apply path, separate from converter charge, and relies on seals and bushings. A TCC control valve, stroked by the TCC solenoid, acts as the gateway between the release and apply circuits. A TCC regulator valve sets apply pressure. A converter feed limit valve caps converter feed pressure. Worn TCC apply or regulator bores bleed apply oil and cause slip codes.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, A.11}: Two-path lockup converter: release oil flows through the center of the turbine shaft and exits between the TCC piston and the cover. Apply pressure supplies the clamping force on the piston. A two-path design needs good clutch friction to separate release and apply pressure. A three-path design relies on seals and/or bushings to control apply and adds a separate TC charge path. Two-path control typically uses three to four valves. The TC control valve is the gateway for apply and release oil and is stroked by TC solenoid output to begin regulating slip. Lockup has three stages: full release, controlled/modulated apply, and full apply. — [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part IV: Converter Clutch Control"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/713-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-iv-converter-clutch-control); [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Converter Clutch Control Part 1"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/hydraulics-fundamentals-converter-clutch-control-part-1/) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80/90] {A.14}: The converter feed limit valve, at rest, lets PR-valve output flow freely to the TCC control valve. Oil also reaches the valve's inboard end through an orifice. As pressure builds, the valve strokes and cuts converter feed back to its limit pressure. This back-and-forth movement is what limits it. — [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part VI: Converter Limit Valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/844-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-vi-converter-limit-valves) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, pattern; kit pages are unit-specific] {A.14, A.17}: The TCC regulator valve controls TCC apply pressure. Both the apply valve and the regulator valve can cause slip codes. A worn apply valve can return to unapply even while the TCC solenoid stays energized. The apply valve causes larger slip-RPM swings as the converter cycles from apply to release. The regulator valve causes more gradual slip that is sensitive to load and heat. Apply-valve bore wear lets regulated apply oil exhaust, which lowers apply pressure and makes the clutch slip. Regulator-bore wear reduces apply pressure, or the valve's contact damages the TCC solenoid. — Sonnax kit pages: [TCC Apply Valve Kit 84754-22K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2556-tcc-apply-valve-kit); [TCC Regulator Valve Kit 55211-04K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2187-tcc-regulator-valve-kit); [TCC Regulated Apply Valve Kit 84754-34K](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/2561-tcc-regulated-apply-valve-kit) (snippet; blended summary)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM] {A.14, A.11}: The TCC failing to release (stalling at a stop) is common on GM units. It is usually a stuck TCC solenoid, but it can also come from a converter clutch apply valve stuck in apply, a clogged solenoid exhaust orifice, or a sticking TCC control, TCC regulator apply, torque-signal regulator, or PR valve. Residual pressure from internal cross-leaks can hold the TCC release pressure switch open with a normally working TCC, setting a GM P1887. — [Sonnax, "Beyond 1870: GM Converter Codes P0741, P0742 and 1887"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/120-beyond-1870-gm-converter-codes-p0741-p0742-and-1887); [LiveAbout, "Diagnosing GM converter lock-up problems"](https://www.liveabout.com/diagnosing-gm-converter-lock-up-problems-262653) (snippet; the P0742 cause list also appears in a GM-derived DTC page, [ls2.com P0742](https://www.ls2.com/boggs/a4/DTC%20P0742%205.7.htm))

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: The slip-pattern split is useful for Tech A/Tech B reasoning. Abrupt slip cycling suggests the apply/control valve. Slip that builds gradually with heat and load suggests the regulator or bore wear, or converter clutch wear.
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: In a two-pass unit, a leak that lets release oil in during apply (or apply oil bleed away) reduces the net clamping pressure difference, so the unit slips while the solenoid command looks normal. A worn turbine-shaft seal or bushing in a three-pass unit does the same thing through a different path.
- INFERRED: The prompt's "TCC enable valve" is a unit-specific name. No snippet defined it generically. Treat it as a valve that permits TCC feed only in the correct range or gear. [Needs SI]

## 5. Accumulators and accumulator control valves

### Takeaway
An accumulator is a spring-loaded piston teed into an apply circuit. It absorbs the initial pressure rise so clutch torque builds at a controlled rate, which acts as a shock absorber for shift feel. Accumulator control valves, and later solenoids, vary the backpressure with throttle or load. Many GM 6-, 8- and 10-speed clutch-to-clutch units have dropped traditional accumulators because the pressure-control solenoids shape the apply directly. Failure modes: a broken spring, or worn or hard seals, lets the piston stroke too fast, giving harsh shifts.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, A.17}: Accumulators were long the primary shift-feel control, acting as a shock absorber for the pressure applying a clutch, band or brake. In older hydraulic 3- and 4-speeds they were large pistons and springs with additional control valves. Electronics later added solenoids to accumulator circuits. Many newer 6- and 8-speeds no longer need traditional accumulators because the computer and solenoids control shifts directly. — [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part II: Accumulators & Shift Feel"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/675-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-ii-accumulators-shift-feel); [Transmission Digest, same title](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/hydraulics-fundamentals-accumulators-shift-feel/) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: The rate of clutch pressure rise sets the rate of clutch torque rise. Too rapid a rise gives a hard shift. — [Sonnax Part II](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/675-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-ii-accumulators-shift-feel); consistent with accumulator patents such as [US 6162147](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6162147) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.17, A.14}: A spring holds the piston at the top of its bore when the circuit is idle. As circuit pressure rises the piston strokes, absorbing pressure and softening and delaying the shift. A faulty accumulator or broken spring causes harsh shifts. An N-D hard engagement points to the N-D accumulator, and a hard 1-2 points to the 1-2 accumulator. — [Free ASE Study Guides, "Hard shifting automatic transmission faulty accumulator"](https://www.freeasestudyguides.com/transmission-shift-troubleshooting.html) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 4L60-E] {A.17}: The 4L60-E is known for broken 1-2 accumulator springs and worn or cracked plastic pistons. Worn or hardened o-rings let the piston move faster than intended. — [Free ASE Study Guides](https://www.freeasestudyguides.com/transmission-shift-troubleshooting.html) and enthusiast threads e.g. [SSR Fanatic 4L60E mushy 1-2](https://www.ssrfanatic.com/threads/i-found-the-reason-for-my-4l60e-transmission-mushy-1-2-upshifts-accumulator.271563/) (snippet; forums are low authority)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 4L60/700-R4] {A.14}: Sonnax publishes an accumulator-valve code guide for the 4L60/700-R4/4L60-E. That shows the accumulator valve (the regulator that sets accumulator backpressure) is calibrated per application. — [Sonnax, "GM 4L60/700-R4/4L60-E Accumulator Valve Codes"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1061-gm-4l60-700-r4-4l60-e-accumulator-valve-codes-deciphering-the-alphabet-soup-of-shift-feel) (title/snippet only)
- CONFLICT / OPEN: One snippet said a stiffer spring gives a softer shift and a softer spring a firmer one, "with the piston on top of the springs." Whether spring rate firms or softens the shift depends on whether the spring is on the apply side or the backpressure side in that unit. Do not generalize. — [Mark VIII 1-2 accumulator paper](http://www.markviii.org/LOD2/1_2_Accumulator_paper.htm) / forum results (snippet; low authority)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: On a hydraulic diagram, a shift-feel complaint on only one shift points to that shift's accumulator, its control valve, or its feed orifice or check ball. A complaint on all shifts points to line pressure or the accumulator-control signal (TV/EPC).
- INFERRED [GM clutch-to-clutch] {A.14}: On 6L80-class units, the functional replacement for "accumulator" diagnosis is the clutch regulator valve and pressure-control solenoid (section 7), plus adapts (see `shift_phases_adapts.md`).

## 6. Orifices and check balls: apply vs exhaust timing

### Takeaway
Separator-plate orifices meter oil flow to set apply timing and damp valve oscillation. A check ball in parallel with an orifice gives different apply and exhaust rates: one direction flows through the orifice only, the other unseats the ball for free flow. A missing, shrunken or misplaced check ball, or an eroded orifice, changes engagement or release timing. In the worst case it removes a range entirely (6L80 examples).

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: Separator-plate orifices are precisely sized holes that restrict flow. They work with valves and check balls for timing and to prevent valve fluctuation. Enlarging a clutch feed orifice speeds engagement and firms the shift. Oversizing it gives harsh shifts. — [Sonnax, "Another Day at the Orifice: Separator Plate Modification Considerations"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/668-another-day-at-the-orifice-separator-plate-modification-considerations) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, example] {A.14, A.17}: Oil pressure seats the check ball against the separator plate, and flow is routed through a small orifice, which cushions the apply. When wear enlarges the hole, exhaust flow increases and the band releases too fast, causing downshift clunks. When the hole gets large enough, the ball sticks in it, forcing all exhaust through the small orifice and slowing the release. — [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Check Valves"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/tasc-force-tips-hydraulics-fundamentals-check-valves/); [Sonnax, "Part VIII: Check Valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/881-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-viii-check-valves) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80/90] {A.14, C-area overhaul}: A shrunken, out-of-place No. 1 check ball can cause no-forward. An out-of-place No. 5 check ball can cause no-reverse. A Type 2 separator plate on a Type 1 valve body causes no-forward. The correct plate uses seven check balls. A later design added a #8 check ball. — [Sonnax, "GM 6L45/50, 6L80/90 No Movement Conditions"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1216-gm-6l45-50-6l80-90-no-movement-conditions-analysis-repair-tips); [Transmission Digest, same article](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/gm-6l-series-no-movement-conditions-analysis-repair-tips/); [Sonnax/TD, "#8 Checkball for 6L80/90"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/location-and-function-of-new-8-checkball-for-6l80-90/) (snippet)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: Diagram rule of thumb. A ball-plus-orifice pair means apply and exhaust are timed differently. Identify which direction seats the ball: that direction flows through the orifice only (slow), and the other flows freely (fast). A missing ball makes both directions fast, which on the apply side gives a harsh engagement. A missing orifice restriction likewise gives harsh or fast. A plugged orifice gives slow/delayed apply or slow release (possible tie-up or flare-free "drag").
- INFERRED [Universal] {C-area}: Check-ball count and location are unit and design-level specific, so they need the SI/valve-body layout, never memory.

## 7. Clutch-to-clutch units (GM 6L80): actuator feed limit, clutch select, clutch regulator, compensator feed, and default gears

### Takeaway
On clutch-to-clutch units, the actuator feed limit (AFL, or solenoid regulator/modulator) valve makes a regulated, lower-than-line feed for the solenoids. Variable pressure-control solenoids stroke clutch regulator valves (often through gain or boost valves), which regulate line into each clutch. On/off solenoids stroke clutch select valves, which route regulated oil to the correct clutch. A compensator feed regulator supplies balance oil to rotating drums to cancel centrifugal apply. With a total loss of electrical control, the 6L80 goes to maximum line, TCC off, and a hydraulic default gear (3rd or 5th depending on the gear at failure), with reverse and park still available.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80; also 4L60E/4L80E] {A.14, A.9}: The AFL valve regulates the oil pressure supplied to the control solenoids and is also called a solenoid regulator valve. A worn or stuck AFL/solenoid modulator valve can cause erratic pressures, starve or flood the solenoids, solenoid codes, wrong-gear starts, clutch/band failure, and harsh or soft shifts. On 6-speeds, slips, flares and even loss of 4th-6th gears can indicate solenoid-regulator failure. On 4L60E/4L80E, worn AFL bores cause low actuator feed and low line pressure. A large reaction area on the spring end side-loads the valve, and high-frequency movement wears the bore. — [Sonnax, "Hydraulics Fundamentals Part VII: AFL/Solenoid Modulator Valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/847-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-vii-afl-solenoid-modulator-valves); [Transmission Digest, same](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/tasc-force-tips-3/); [Sonnax, "Uncovering the root cause of pressure complaints: solenoid modulator / AFL valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1208-uncovering-the-root-cause-of-pressure-complaints-the-critical-role-of-solenoid-modulator-afl-valves); [Sonnax AFL Valve Kit 104740-47K (6L80)](https://www.sonnax.com/parts/5073-actuator-feed-limit-valve-kit) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal for late-model units] {A.14, A.9}: Later-model units control shift apply and feel with clutch regulator valves, their actuators, and often a valve controlling bias (balance) pressure. The TCM ramps current on a variable-feed solenoid to raise the signal. That signal passes through a gain valve and strokes the clutch regulator valve to open clutch feed. In clutch-to-clutch units, pressure-control-solenoid pressure acts on a clutch regulator valve and a clutch boost valve, letting the TCM directly control both apply and release pressure at each clutch. — [Transmission Digest, "Hydraulics Fundamentals: Clutch Regulator Valves"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/tasc-force-tips-hydraulics-fundamentals-clutch-regulator-valves/); [Sonnax, "Part IX: Clutch Regulator Valves: Controlling Shift & Apply Feel"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/929-hydraulics-fundamentals-part-ix-clutch-regulator-valves-controlling-shift-apply-feel) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80/90] {A.14, C-area}: The clutch select valves direct flow. A backwards or improperly installed clutch select valve can cause loss of forward, reverse, or both (valves go in first, springs outboard). A stuck clutch select valve can cause the same no-movement condition, and line pressure is typically normal in that case. The 3-5-R clutch regulator valve is known to stick, causing no-reverse and shift complaints. — [Sonnax, "GM 6L45/50, 6L80/90 No Movement Conditions"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1216-gm-6l45-50-6l80-90-no-movement-conditions-analysis-repair-tips); [Sonnax, "Addressing GM 6L80, 6L90 No Reverse Condition"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1613-addressing-gm-6l80-6l90-no-reverse-condition) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L series] {A.14}: The compensator feed regulator valve supplies regulated pressure to the balance side of rotating clutch drums. That counteracts unintended apply from centrifugal force on residual apply-chamber oil and simplifies the TCM's apply calculations. — [TransGo 6L80-CFR product page](https://transgo.com/product-details/6l80-cfr/) (snippet; aftermarket vendor, but the mechanism is standard)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80/90] {A.9, A.14}: If the electronic control system or any component in the control solenoid body/TCM is disabled, the unit goes to fail-safe. The line-pressure control solenoid goes OFF, giving maximum line pressure, and the TCC solenoid goes OFF, giving no TCC apply. If the failure happens in 1st-3rd the unit defaults to 3rd. If it happens in 4th-6th it defaults to 5th. — [trucktransdiag.com, "6L80/90 technical information" PDF](https://www.trucktransdiag.com/downloads/6L80_technical_tips.pdf); [ATSG 6-speed manual (mirror)](https://atoc.ru/uploads/manual/5fb79aebb40df.pdf) (snippet; blended summary; verify in GM SI, which should hold equivalent "default gear" text)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, general] {A.9}: Reverse usually still works in limp mode because on most units it is hydraulically defaulted. — [GearboxInsider, "Transmission limp mode"](https://gearboxinsider.com/limp-mode-transmission-causes-and-fix/) (snippet; **low authority**)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal, electronic] {A.9, A.14}: Why "loss of power = max line": GM PC/EPC solenoids on these units are "normally high" (off = maximum pressure). So an unplugged solenoid body gives firm engagements and a fixed gear. That is a hydraulic fail-safe designed in, not a mechanical fault. [Needs SI per unit: not every PC solenoid is normally high]
- INFERRED {A.14}: "No forward (or no reverse) with normal line pressure" on a 6L80 points to routing (clutch select valve, check ball, separator plate, clutch regulator) rather than supply (pump, PR, filter). This is the clutch-to-clutch version of "low pressure in one range only" (section 10).
- INFERRED {A.9}: The AFL valve is a common upstream cause of multiple solenoid-performance DTCs at once. A worn AFL bore can make good solenoids look bad.

## 8. Converter-out → cooler → lube circuit: why a restricted cooler causes overheating, lube starvation and bushing failure

### Takeaway
On most units, converter-out oil (after passing through the converter) goes to the cooler and returns as the lube supply for bushings, planetaries and thrust washers. A restriction anywhere in the cooler path (lines, radiator cooler, auxiliary cooler, drain-back fitting, or a misplaced bushing covering the to-cooler port) cuts flow. The result is heat (less cooling, and converter oil not exchanged) and lube starvation, which wears bushings and planetaries. It can also upset converter and valve control. Test cooler flow and head pressure before condemning the internals.

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, B.8}: Cooler restrictions can be at the drain-back fitting, the auxiliary or radiator cooler, or the lines. They not only reduce flow, which shows up as lube failures, but also hurt converter and valve control. Checking cooler flow and head pressure together is the best way to isolate the problem. If you cannot measure flow, at least check head pressure. — [Sonnax/Transmission Digest, "46-47RH/RE Cooler and Converter Flow"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/46-47rh-re-cooler-and-converter-flow/); [Sonnax version](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/199-46-47rh-re-cooler-and-converter-flow) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: If the priority/relief valve stays out of balance for extended periods, converter and lube oil is restricted. Past a critical threshold the converter and/or transmission fails. — [Sonnax, "4R100 Repeated Converter Failure"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/184-4r100-repeated-converter-failure); [Transmission Digest version](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/4r100-repeated-converter-failure/) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM THM 350] {C-area}: A bushing driven too deep into the bellhousing/pump can partly block the to-cooler passage, which acts as a cooler restriction. — search summary citing [Transmission Digest back-issue index](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/september-2008-issue/) / [Sonnax oil-flow anatomy article](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/131-anatomy-of-a-transmission-oil-flow-in-the-pump-pr-converter-cooler-lube-circuits) (snippet; exact source article not separable)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, B.8}: Low cooler flow causes bushing wear. Adequate lube supply is necessary to prevent catastrophic planetary failure. Do a lube/cooler flow test and replace the cooler as needed. Check the external cooler before dropping the pan, because a cooler restriction can cause backpressure with no internal mechanical failure. — [Sonnax, "Understanding transmission cooler control valves"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1268-understanding-cooler-control-valves-what-thermal-vs-pressure-control-means-for-your-build); [Sun Transmissions pressure-testing guide](https://suntransmissions.com/blogs/news/transmission-pressure-testing-guide-diagnosing-hydraulic-failures) (snippet; the second is a commercial blog)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: The Sonnax "Anatomy of a Transmission" article is titled for exactly this path: pump → PR → converter → cooler → lube. — [Sonnax, "Anatomy of a Transmission: Oil Flow in the Pump-PR-Converter-Cooler-Lube Circuits"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/131-anatomy-of-a-transmission-oil-flow-in-the-pump-pr-converter-cooler-lube-circuits) (title only; fetch blocked)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14, B.8}: Chain of failure: restricted cooler → converter-out backpressure and less flow → hotter fluid (converter heat not carried away) → thinner fluid, which also worsens bore-wear leaks (see section 1) → reduced lube flow → bushing, thrust and planetary wear → possibly contaminated fluid that plugs more of the cooler. This is why flushing or replacing the cooler after an internal failure is standard practice.
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: Because converter feed often comes off the PR valve's first "overflow" stage, a leak or restriction upstream (low pump output, PR stuck) can also starve the converter and lube even with a clean cooler.
- INFERRED [Unit-specific] {B.8}: Some units have thermostatic cooler bypass valves (Sonnax covers thermal vs pressure cooler control). A stuck bypass can mimic a restricted cooler (runs hot) or a cold-running unit. [Needs SI]

## 9. Legacy TV cable, vacuum modulator and governor principles

### Takeaway
The vacuum modulator converts manifold vacuum into a TV/modulator signal. High vacuum (light load) lowers line pressure. Low vacuum (heavy load, a leak, or a disconnected hose) raises line pressure and gives firmer, later shifts. A TV cable does the same job mechanically from throttle position. On a 700R4/200-4R the TV cable sets both line pressure and shift points. Too loose gives low pressure and early, soft shifts, which burn the unit up. Too tight gives high pressure and late, harsh shifts. Governor pressure supplies the road-speed side of the shift-valve balance (section 3).

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, legacy] {A.14, B.6}: The modulator controls line pressure. At full vacuum it reduces line pressure, and at WOT (low vacuum) it increases it. Low vacuum generally gives firm/hard shifts. The modulator should soften shifts with vacuum applied and firm them as vacuum bleeds off under acceleration. The TV valve replaced the modulator partly so engine age and vacuum leaks would not shift the shift points. — [Pirate4x4, "LQ9 to TH400 issues: vacuum modulator"](https://www.pirate4x4.com/threads/lq9-to-th400-issues-vacuum-modulator.1015272/); [Third Generation F-Body forum, TV vs modulator](https://www.thirdgen.org/forums/transmissions-drivetrain/154124-tv-vs-vacuum-modulator.html); [Core Transmission Parts, TH350 modulator](https://coretransmissionparts.com/blogs/news/th350-vacuum-modulator-adjust-and-replace) (snippet; mostly forums, low authority; the mechanism agrees across all three)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 700R4/200-4R] {B.6, A.14}: A TV cable that is too loose gives soft, "stacked" shifts at lower pressure and early shift points, and a loose or disconnected cable will burn up the transmission. Too tight gives higher pressure and later shifts, and if it is very high, possible second-gear starts. On these units the TV cable controls both shift feel (apply pressure) and shift points, plus part-throttle and detent downshifts. Adjust it with a pressure gauge. — [Gearstar, "What happens if your 700R4 TV cable is out of adjustment"](https://gearstar.com/blog/what-happens-if-your-700r4-tv-cable-is-out-of-adjustment); [Summit Racing 700R4/2004R TV cable instructions PDF](https://static.summitracing.com/global/images/instructions/700r4%20cable%20adjustment%20instructions.pdf) (snippet; two commercial sources agree; verify the "second-gear starts" claim)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal, legacy]: Governor mechanism: see section 3 (BYU-I textbook snippet and patents).

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal, legacy] {A.14}: The modulator diaphragm is also a leak path. A ruptured modulator diaphragm pulls ATF into the intake: low fluid, white exhaust smoke, and a vacuum line wet with ATF. This is common textbook knowledge but was **not** confirmed by any snippet in this session, so it is OPEN until verified.
- INFERRED {A.14}: The brief's statement "low vacuum → higher modulator pressure" is confirmed (snippet, multiple forums). The TV misadjustment symptoms in the brief are confirmed for the 700R4/200-4R (two commercial sources).

## 10. Sticking valves, worn bores, and "low pressure in one range only"

### Takeaway
Valve-body faults split into two kinds. Sticking (varnish, debris) gives intermittent or temperature-dependent wrong-gear, no-shift and TCC symptoms. Bore wear gives cross-leaks: low or erratic pressure, soft shifts, TCC slip, and solenoid-performance codes that worsen hot. Sonnax documents wear-prone bores per unit (AFL, PR/boost, TCC regulator/apply) and verifies them by vacuum testing. The reasoning for "low in one range only": if line pressure is normal in some ranges and low in one, the pump, PR and filter are proven good. The leak is in a circuit that is fed only in the low range (that range's clutch/servo, its seals, or a valve or bore that only sees oil in that range).

### Cited Findings
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14, A.17}: Wear of valve bores and spools in modern lightweight valve bodies causes shift-control issues that depend on oil temperature (thick when cold, thin when hot). A sharp valve land edge acts as a wiper to push particles before they embed and stick the valve. A worn valve or bore produces incorrect pressures. Vacuum testing (done dry) gives a repeatable number for valve-to-bore leakage: the bigger the leak, the lower the vacuum reading. It tests captive circuits before disassembly. — [Sonnax, "Transmission testing & repairs: make sure you're vacuum testing"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/949-transmission-testing-repairs-it-s-not-business-as-usual-so-make-sure-you-re-vacuum-testing); [Sonnax, "Vacuum testing for leakage"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/121-vacuum-testing-for-leakage); [Sonnax, "Valve body inspection and rebuilding"](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/172-valve-body-inspection-and-rebuilding) (snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific examples] {A.17}: Sonnax publishes unit-specific vacuum-test guides, e.g. the GM 4L60-E/4L65-E/4L70-E and ZF6HP. That shows wear-prone bore locations are catalogued per unit, not universal. — [Sonnax 4L60-E Vacuum Test Guide](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/441-gm-4l60-e-4l65-e-4l70-e-vacuum-test-guide); [Sonnax ZF6HP Vacuum Test Guide](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/437-zf6hp21-28-34-gen-2-vacuum-test-guide) (title/snippet)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: When reverse alone is low, suspect a reverse-circuit-specific problem such as valve-body wear. A subtle leak in a containment area may be masked by "max" line pressure. Check line pressure in reverse, 3rd, 4th and manual 1st against the chart, both low and high side. — [Transmission Digest, "Diagnosis with Pressure Gauges"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/diagnosis-with-pressure-gauges/); [Sonnax/TD, "Diagnosing the elusive no reverse with the 4L80-E"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/diagnosing-the-elusive-no-reverse-with-the-4l80-e/); [Gears, "Diagnosing Pressure Trouble Codes"](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/diagnosing-pressure-trouble-codes/) (snippet; blended summary)
- DOCUMENTED [Universal] {A.14}: To isolate a circuit leak, use two gauges, one on line pressure and one on the clutch or servo circuit, read at the same time. A difference beyond spec indicates a leak in that circuit. (The snippet quoted example thresholds of "more than 10%" and "usually 8-15 psi". These are **not** specs. Use the SI or manufacturer value.) A captive wet-air test (pan off, manual valve in the range, regulated air into the line tap) is another isolation method. — [Transmission Digest, "Diagnosis with Pressure Gauges"](https://www.transmissiondigest.com/diagnosis-with-pressure-gauges/); [Free ASE Study Guides, pressure tests](https://www.freeasestudyguides.com/automatic-transmission-pressure-tests.html) (snippet; the air-pressure value in the snippet is omitted here as a non-spec)
- DOCUMENTED [Unit-specific: GM 6L80] {A.14}: A stuck clutch select valve causes no movement with typically normal line pressure (see section 7). — [Sonnax 6L no-movement article](https://www.sonnax.com/tech_resources/1216-gm-6l45-50-6l80-90-no-movement-conditions-analysis-repair-tips) (snippet)

### Inferences
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: The core diagram-reasoning method is subtraction:
  1. Line pressure low in **all** ranges: common supply (filter/pickup, pump, PR/boost, EPC signal, AFL on some units, or a big common leak).
  2. Normal in some ranges, low in **one**: trace which clutch, servo or passage is fed only in that range on the hydraulic diagram and the apply chart. The leak lives there (piston seal, sealing ring, case/pump passage, that range's valve bore, check ball, or gasket).
  3. **Normal pressure but no engagement**: routing (manual valve, clutch select or shift valve stuck, separator plate or check ball), or a mechanical failure (broken clutch, sprag).
  4. Pressure **drops only when a specific shift happens**: leak in the newly applied circuit.
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.14}: Varnish or debris sticking tends to be intermittent and cold-worse, or to follow contamination events. Bore wear tends to be hot-worse and progressive. This gives a Tech A/Tech B-style discriminator, though real units may show both.
- INFERRED [Universal] {A.17}: "TCC slip + soft shifts + multiple pressure-control DTCs, worse hot" fits upstream bore wear (AFL, PR, TCC regulator) more than several failed solenoids at once.

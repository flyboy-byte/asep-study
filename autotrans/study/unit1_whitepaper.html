01

Power Flow & Planetary Gear Fundamentals

A manual transmission physically connects gears with a sleeve/synchronizer assembly. An automatic transmission does the same job a different way: a clutch pack, pushed by hydraulic pressure, locks the gear connection instead. The ability to "stall" while in gear belongs to the torque converter, not the transmission — once a gear is engaged, turning the input shaft mechanically turns the output shaft.

1.1Simple planetary gear set

Three members, always in mesh:

MemberRole
Sun gearCenter of the set.
Planet carrier (+ pinions)Holds the pinions, which revolve around the sun gear and stay in constant mesh with both the sun and the ring gear.
Ring (annulus) gearOutermost member, internal teeth.
Memorize

One member is held (reaction), one is driven (input), one is the output. Two held/driven together = direct drive. None held = neutral. More planet pinions adds strength, not ratio change.

1.2General planetary rules

ResultCondition
NeutralNo driving member or reaction member
Forward direct drive, 1:1Two members are driven
Forward reductionCarrier is the output
Forward overdriveCarrier is the input
Reverse, reduction or overdriveCarrier is the reaction member
Forward or reverse overdriveSun gear is the output
Forward or reverse reductionSun gear is the input
Reverse rotationOne external gear drives another external gear
Same-direction rotationAn external gear drives an internal gear, or vice versa

Identify the actual input, reaction, and output members in the diagram before applying these rules.

1.3Calculating gear ratios

A simple planetary set produces seven ratios plus neutral, depending on which member is driven and which is held: ring held/sun driven → reduction; ring held/carrier driven → overdrive (reciprocal); sun held/ring driven → reduction; sun held/carrier driven → overdrive (reciprocal); carrier held/sun driven → reverse reduction; carrier held/ring driven → reverse overdrive (reciprocal). You should be able to work through which member is doing what and get the ratio type right using the reference material — not necessarily recite every formula cold.

1.4Compound gear sets

SetBuilt fromUsed for
SimpsonTwo ring gears + two planet carriers sharing one sun gear4- and 5-speed transmissions
RavigneauxOne carrier with two sets of planet gears + two sun gears + one ring gearCombined with a simple set for 6–8 speeds
LePelletierA Ravigneaux set + a simple planetary set6/7/8-speed (Ford 6R60/6R80, GM 6L80E)

In a LePelletier train, the ring gear is always an input and the sun gear is always a reaction member (splined to the pump), so the simple set is always in reduction — it feeds a reduced-speed input into the Ravigneaux set, which has three possible inputs and two reaction members.

Instructor note

You don't need to memorize the full power flow. You do need the basic concepts — what a planetary set is, how ratios are determined, and the difference between gear-set families.

1.5Why more ratios (8/9/10-speed)

More ratios → lower first gear (better launch) and higher overdrive ratios (lower engine speed at highway speed → better fuel economy, lower emissions). Eight/nine/ten-speed gear sets are hard to classify into one simple family — they combine more than one compound set. Always check service information for the actual design rather than assuming a family.

1.6Synchronous vs. nonsynchronous shifts

TypeWhat it meansUses a one-way clutch?
Synchronous (overlap / clutch-to-clutch)The new driving/reaction member must be timed with the release of the old one. A band must release and reapply at an exact moment.No
Nonsynchronous (asynchronous / freewheel)A one-way clutch self-releases (overruns) the instant the next member applies — no timing needed.Yes
Memorize

Synchronous shifts don't use a one-way clutch; nonsynchronous shifts do. Mistiming a clutch-to-clutch shift risks applying two ratios at once — a very harsh shift, or a flare the other way. Most modern transmissions are fully synchronous.

02

Apply Devices, Shift Quality & the Illustrated 10-Speed

2.1Clutch packs

Alternating frictions (fiber plates) and steels (separator plates), finished with a pressure plate retained by a snap ring. Unapplied plates need clearance to stay cool, avoid drag, and let fluid pass between them. Fluid feeds from the shaft into the drum, sealed with sealing rings. A controlled release matters as much as apply timing for synchronous shifts — most transmissions use a return spring pack, though some use fluid on both sides of the piston when release needs to be faster than a spring alone.

2.2Bands and servos

A band is a lined metal strip that wraps a drum to prevent it from rotating — rigid (thick) or flexible (thin/flex) types. A servo is the hydraulic assembly that applies the band, using pressure to move a piston that tightens it.

2.3One-way clutches

TypeHow it works
Roller clutchRoller bearings lock into a ramped outer race.
Sprag clutchHourglass-shaped sprags between smooth inner/outer races; major diameter > race gap, minor diameter < race gap.
Mechanical diodeSpring-loaded rectangular struts (pocket plate) engage notches (notch plate); stronger than a roller or sprag design — think of a ratchet wrench.

A one-way clutch applies or holds only when needed and self-releases (overruns) when not — that's what makes a nonsynchronous shift possible without timing.

2.4Garage shifts vs. power shifts

  • Garage shift — a neutral-to-drive or neutral-to-reverse shift made at rest. Slower and less severe (3–4 seconds), but needs high static friction since it's moving a stopped vehicle. Some apply devices are pre-applied at rest so only the last one is needed to complete the shift.
  • Power shift — a clutch applied while the vehicle is already moving, under load. Needs a high dynamic coefficient of friction.

2.5Shift quality

Smooth power shifts require precise timing between the outgoing and incoming apply device. Flare, harsh engagement, bump, or unusual noise (squeak/squawk/shriek) are all shift-quality faults.

Rule

Accumulator: tied hydraulically to a clutch/band servo. Absorbs fluid during pressure buildup, slowing the rise and lengthening lockup time. Unlike a brake accumulator, it stores no backup pressure — smoothing is its only job. If the piston bottoms out before the shift finishes, a sudden pressure spike causes a slide-bump shift (smooth start, harsh finish).

Wave plate (Belleville/cushion plate) — a non-flat plate that adds shift smoothing. Many transmissions skip it and rely on accumulators/orifices plus, on electronic units, solenoid cycling instead.

Pattern

High reduction (Reverse ≈ 4.87:1, 1st ≈ 4.70:1) → decreasing reduction through 6th (≈1.28:1) → 7th direct drive (1.000:1) → increasing overdrive through 10th (≈0.64:1). OWC holds in 1st–2nd, overruns 3rd–7th, unused in 8th–10th (no element to overrun for free coasting — engine compression slows the car on lift-throttle until it downshifts).

Diagnostic use of this chart: if two affected ranges share an applied element, that element is a candidate cause. If an unaffected range also depends on that same element, more evidence is needed — pressure tests, speed-sensor data, ratio-error info, and the exact SI chart separate a hydraulic, mechanical, or control cause. Always pull the exact chart for the vehicle/calibration — calibration changes can make a clutch's functional name inaccurate.

03

Hydraulic System & Electronic Controls

3.1Hydraulic system — purpose

  1. Applies clutches/bands, controlling power flow.
  2. Transmits enough force to fully apply control units and prevent slippage.
  3. Maintains flow through the torque converter.
  4. Lubricates and cools the gear train.

Pascal's Law: pressure is transmitted equally and undiminished in every direction through a fluid. Force ÷ area = pressure (e.g. 10 lb on a 1 sq-in piston = 10 PSI), and that pressure transmits without loss throughout the system (same principle used in brake hydraulics).

Rule

The pump does not create pressure by itself — pressure comes from resistance to flow. An automatic transmission always uses an internal pump: internal/external gear (crescent/gear), Gerotor (rotor), or vane — same underlying principle, an expanding cavity.

Variable displacement pumps are still positive-displacement (fixed volume per revolution) but can change that displacement — typically by moving the vane housing to change the pumping-chamber size.

3.2Electronic controls — evolution

Older transmissions used governor pressure (100% road-speed-based) working against throttle valve pressure (rises with throttle position) — the more throttle, the more governor pressure needed to trigger a shift. Modern transmissions replace that hydraulic logic with the TCM, which sits on the CAN network.

3.3TCM inputs (direct)

  • Input and output shaft speed sensors
  • Transmission range sensor
  • Fluid pressure sensors
  • Fluid temperature sensor
  • Shift and pressure control solenoids

Shift timing/quality and TCC apply timing/quality are largely software-driven — the same hardware can behave differently across vehicle variants purely through calibration, and some TCMs can be reprogrammed in the field.

Transmission Range (TR) switch — also called the manual lever position (MLP) sensor. Mounted on the case, attached to the shifter, tells the TCM which range the driver selected.

3.4Speed sensors — ISS and OSS

SensorAlso calledMeasuresUsed for
Input Speed Sensor (ISS)Turbine Speed Sensor (TSS)Input/turbine shaft speed (≈ engine speed)Line pressure, shift timing, TCC slip speed, gear ratio
Output Speed Sensor (OSS)Vehicle Speed (VS) sensorOutput shaft speedSpeedometer, cruise control, shift timing, vehicle speed, fault detection

Both are hall-effect sensors that can use a clutch drum or housing itself as the reluctor (trigger wheel) — not always a dedicated toothed wheel.

3.5Pressure switches and pressure logic

Most pressure sensors are transducers (variable resistance proportional to pressure) — e.g. the line pressure sensor (LPS). Pressure switches at the valve body are simpler: on/off, either normally open (N.O.) or normally closed (N.C.). The TCM reads the on/off pattern of multiple switches (routed through the pressure switch manifold, PSM) to determine which gear is actually engaged, and uses that to adjust line pressure, TCC apply, and shift solenoid operation.

6L80 example

Teacher's analogy: "like a keyboard switch." The fluid pressure switches are normally closed — current flows when no pressure is present. When pressure reaches the switch, it moves a diaphragm/piston/disk that opens the circuit (current stops).

3.6Temperature sensors

Most are NTC thermistors (negative temperature coefficient — resistance drops as temperature rises). The TFT (transmission fluid temperature) sensor works this way: cold fluid = high resistance = high signal voltage; as fluid warms, resistance and signal voltage drop. The TCM uses this for shift quality and TCC apply quality across the operating temperature range.

3.7Sensors shared with the PCM

Accelerator pedal position (APP), throttle position (TP, a potentiometer), engine coolant temperature (ECT, a thermistor), and MAP/MAF (engine load) all feed both the PCM and TCM so engine and transmission operation stay coordinated. The brake switch is a transmission input too — it can affect torque converter clutch state (detail covered later in the course).

3.8Solenoids

An electronic transmission shifts by turning solenoids on/off, which control the hydraulic pressure operating the shift valves or the torque converter clutch.

SolenoidTypeFunction
EPC (Electronic Pressure Control) / PCS / VFS / force motorNormally closed, PWM (~300–600 Hz)Controls the pressure regulator valve → line pressure. Higher duty cycle = more current = lower pressure. Max current ≈ 1 amp.
Shift solenoids (e.g. 6L80)Normally closed, 3-port, ON/OFFWork with pressure control solenoids to operate shift/clutch regulator valves; TCM picks the on/off combination for the commanded gear.
TCC PC solenoidNormally-low, precision regulatorControls TCC apply/release pressure; can "ramp" apply/release for smoother TCC engagement.
Line PC solenoidNormally-high, precision regulator~0.1 A = max line pressure, ~1.0 A = min line pressure — opposite current/pressure relationship from the EPC. Based on throttle position, fluid temp, gear state.

3.9Adaptive learning (TAP)

The TCM/PCM compares commanded shift timing against actual output-speed change. A shift that takes longer than normal (often from clutch pack wear) causes the TCM to start the shift sooner next time — adaptive control/learning, keeping shift duration within a target range based on the driver's own habits. Adapts (TAP) are reset after installing a new transmission or after clutch-to-clutch shift work, and also account for valve body personality, pump variation, and different drive-feel modes (Sport, Tow/Haul, Off-Road, etc.).

Unit 1 Cram Sheet

  • Autotrans vs. mantrans: hydraulic clutch pack replaces the sleeve/synchronizer. Torque converter is what allows stall-in-gear, not the transmission.
  • Planetary set: sun / carrier (+ pinions) / ring. One held (reaction), one driven (input), one output. More pinions = strength, not ratio change.
  • Ratio rule of thumb: carrier output → reduction; carrier input → overdrive; carrier held → reverse. Sun input → reduction; sun output → overdrive.
  • Compound sets: Simpson (1 sun, 2 carriers, 2 rings) → 4–5 spd. Ravigneaux (2 suns, 1 dual carrier, 1 ring) w/ simple set → 6–8 spd. LePelletier = Ravigneaux + simple set (6L80E, 6R60/80).
  • Synchronous = timed, no one-way clutch, mistime → harsh shift/flare. Nonsynchronous = one-way clutch self-releases, no timing needed. Most modern units: synchronous.
  • Clutch pack: alternating frictions/steels + pressure plate + snap ring. Band: wraps a drum, applied by a servo.
  • One-way clutches: roller (rollers on ramped race), sprag (hourglass sprags), mechanical diode (strongest, like a ratchet).
  • Garage shift = at rest, high static friction, can pre-stage devices. Power shift = under load, high dynamic friction.
  • Accumulator: smooths pressure rise only (no backup pressure, unlike brakes). Bottoms out early → slide-bump shift.
  • 10-speed pattern: high reduction → 7th direct 1.000:1 → overdrive through 10th. OWC holds 1st–2nd, overruns 3rd–7th, unused 8th–10th.
  • Pascal's Law: pressure = force ÷ area, transmitted equally everywhere. Pump doesn't create pressure — resistance to flow does.
  • ISS (= TSS) reads input/turbine speed; OSS (= VS sensor) reads output/vehicle speed. Both hall-effect, both can use a clutch drum as the reluctor, both run on a shared 8.3–9.3 V supply. Bad signal from either → DTC.
  • Pressure switches: on/off, N.O./N.C., read as a pattern (pressure logic) to confirm actual gear.
  • Temp sensors: NTC thermistor — resistance drops as temperature rises.
  • EPC solenoid: normally closed, more current = lower pressure. Line PC solenoid: normally high, more current = lower pressure, opposite amp range from EPC.
  • Adaptive learning (TAP): resets after transmission replacement/clutch-to-clutch work; adjusts for wear, valve body, and driver habit; varies by drive mode.

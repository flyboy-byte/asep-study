01

Gear Theory & Power Flow

Think of the transmission as a selective torque multiplier: low gears trade speed for torque, high gears trade torque for speed. Every ratio is that trade — you never get both for free.

1.1Gear ratio basics

  • Reduction — output turns slower than input, torque increases (numerically higher ratio, e.g. 3.5:1).
  • Direct drive — 1:1, input speed equals output speed, no multiplication.
  • Overdrive — output turns faster than input, torque drops below engine output (ratio under 1:1, e.g. 0.8:1).

Ratio math: driven teeth ÷ drive teeth. A 10:7:20 three-gear train still reduces to a 2:1 overall ratio — the idler's own tooth count never enters the final number.

Rule

An idler gear reverses rotational direction only. Even at a different diameter than the drive/driven gears, it does not change the ratio.

1.2Constant-mesh principle

Gear pairs stay always meshed — nothing slides tooth-to-tooth during a shift. What changes is which gear gets locked to its shaft by a synchronizer sleeve.

Memorize

It is never a fixed gear meshed to a fixed gear — always fixed gear ↔ speed gear (a gear that freewheels on the shaft until locked). Only one gearset is locked at a time.

1.3Gear type & lubrication

Helical gears run quiet but generate endthrust (axial load) — shafts need thrust surfaces to control it. Most manual transmissions are splash lubricated: the countershaft dips into the sump, spins, and throws oil onto the mainshaft. There's no oil pump in a typical setup.

02

Synchronizer Assembly & Shift Mechanism

This is the core Unit 2 topic. A complete synchronizer assembly is the hub, sleeve, blocker/synchronizer ring(s), keys/struts, and springs. The speed gear and shift fork work with it but are not part of the assembly itself.

PartWhat it does
HubSplined solid to the shaft — never moves independently of it.
SleeveSlides axially on the hub's external splines; the shift fork moves it.
Speed gearFreewheels on the shaft until locked; has a friction cone and side dog/clutch teeth.
Blocker ringFriction ring with limited rotational play. Contacts the cone; while speeds differ, friction twists it just enough to misalign its teeth from the sleeve — physically blocking premature engagement.
Keys / strutsSpring-loaded, seated under the sleeve. Transfer the sleeve's first motion to the blocker ring, so the ring hits the cone before the sleeve reaches the gear's teeth.
Reframe

The synchronizer isn't "synchronizing the sleeve to the gear." The sleeve/hub are already at shaft speed — friction is bringing the freewheeling gear up or down to match. A question can exploit that distinction.

Material note: paper-coated steel blocker rings wear fast with the wrong fluid — another reason manual-trans fluid type is a real diagnostic item, not "gear oil is gear oil."

2.1Shift mechanism

The shift mechanism transfers the driver's lever motion to the shift forks. Fork-to-sleeve groove clearance matters — if the fork or groove wears on the leading edge, the fork can't push the sleeve far enough to fully seat the dog teeth, and a partially engaged gear (dog teeth are angled like arrowheads) pops back out under load.

Shift shafts are typically named by the gear pairs they control: 1–2, 3–4, 5–R. Older designs may use external linkage instead of internal rails.

2.2Detents & interlocks

  • Detent — spring-loaded ball that holds a shift rail/fork in position. Can be overcome by force; it holds the gear, it doesn't lock it rigidly.
  • Interlock — mechanically prevents two gears being selected at once (commonly pin-and-ball). A mechanical-integrity feature, not a shift-feel feature.
  • Reverse lockout — specifically prevents accidental reverse engagement.
03

Symptom Diagnosis & Transaxle Concepts

3.1Three classic gear/shift symptoms

SymptomWhat's actually happening
Grinding / clashSleeve grinds on dog teeth that can't engage cleanly. One gear → that gear's blocker ring. Every gear → suspect clutch drag before condemning every synchro at once. Damage shows on the dog teeth, not the tooth flanks.
"Block out" — won't shift at allShifts fine engine-off, not running → input shaft is still turning when it shouldn't be (clutch drag / speed mismatch). Ask: is the synchronizer assembly truly stopped, or still rotating?
Pops out of gearFork-to-sleeve groove clearance (top answer) → shaft endplay → worn dog teeth → weak detent → linkage wear.
Rule

"Block out" and all-gear grinding are frequently clutch problems wearing a transmission costume. Rule out clutch drag before opening the case.

3.2Noise diagnosis — the road-speed test

Hold a constant road speed and shift through the gears, listening to how the pitch changes:

  • Pitch tracks road speed in every gear (same in 3rd, 4th, 5th) → the noisy part turns at output-shaft speed → suspect the output shaft/bearing.
  • Pitch is different per gear at the same road speed → the part's speed is changing with ratio → suspect the gearset engaged in that range.

Master question, here and everywhere else in A3: what's rotating, and at what speed, when the noise happens?

3.3Transaxle-specific concepts

In a transaxle, the input shaft ("clutch shaft") is integral to the mainshaft assembly — not a separate piece. That's why it needs no pilot bearing: the whole assembly is self-supported by its own bearings. Compare a longitudinal (non-transaxle) transmission, where the input shaft is separate from the mainshaft and needs a third bearing — the pilot bearing, supported at the crank/flywheel — for that free end.

Because A3 treats the drivetrain as one system, a transaxle's final-drive output gets the same diagnostic treatment as a differential's output — this carries straight into Unit 3.

3.4Torque steer

Pulling to one side under hard acceleration. Most common cause: unequal CV joint operating angles between the two half-shafts (contributing factors: unequal shaft length, a longer shaft twisting more under load).

Mental model

A wobble socket on an extension still transmits torque at an angle, but loses efficiency as the angle grows — past some point it whips and stops working. Equal angles on both sides cancel out symmetrically, even with imperfect mounts. Conceptual cousin: bump steer in suspension geometry.

Unit 2 Cram Sheet

Ratios

Reduction = more torque, less speed (numerically high). Overdrive = less torque, more speed (<1). Direct = 1:1.

Idler changes direction only — never the ratio, even at a different size. 10:7:20 → 2:1 overall.

Constant Mesh

Never fixed-to-fixed — always fixed gear ↔ speed gear (freewheeling until locked). Only one gearset locked at a time.

Helical gears: quiet, but cause endthrust → need thrust surfaces. Splash lubricated: countershaft throws oil onto mainshaft.

Synchronizer Chain

FORK → SLEEVE → KEYS → RING → CONE
→ DOG TEETH (lock)

Hub = fixed to shaft. Ring = blocks until speed matches. It syncs the freewheeling gear to hub speed, not "sleeve to gear." Wrong fluid → fast ring wear.

Shift Mechanism

Detent = holds gear selected, can be overcome by force. Interlock = physically prevents two gears engaging at once.

Fork-to-sleeve groove clearance must be right or the sleeve won't fully seat.

Symptom Logic

One gear grinds → that synchro. All gears grind → clutch drag first.

Won't shift, but fine engine-off → clutch drag. Pops out → fork/sleeve clearance → endplay → dog teeth → detent → linkage.

Noise Test

Constant road speed, shift through gears:

Same pitch every gear → output shaft/bearing. Pitch changes per gear → that gearset.

Always ask: what's rotating, at what speed, when the noise happens?

Transaxle

Input shaft = "clutch shaft," part of mainshaft → no pilot bearing needed.

Longitudinal trans: input shaft separate → needs pilot bearing (3rd support point).

Transaxle final-drive output = treat like a differential (bridges into Unit 3).

Torque Steer

Most common cause: unequal CV joint operating angles (also unequal shaft length/twist).

Equal angles both sides → no torque steer, even with imperfect mounts. Cousin: bump steer.

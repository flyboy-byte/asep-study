"chatgpt audit of the above paragraph,
Mostly correct. Your mental model is good; a few terms/mechanics need tightening.
Fact: The synchronizer hub is splined solidly to the transmission shaft. The sleeve slides axially on external splines of that hub. The speed gear itself normally freewheels on the shaft until the sleeve locks it to the hub/shaft.
The sequence is basically:
Fork moves sleeve → sleeve/keys push the blocker ring toward the gear → blocker ring's friction surface contacts the gear's cone → friction speeds up or slows down the gear relative to the shaft/hub → while there is a speed difference, the blocker ring rotates slightly relative to the hub and deliberately misaligns its teeth → that misalignment physically prevents the sleeve from engaging the gear's clutch/dog teeth → once speeds are close enough, the ring recenters/alignment becomes possible → sleeve passes over the blocker-ring teeth and engages the gear's dog/clutch teeth → gear is now mechanically locked to the shaft.
The biggest correction is this part:
> "dog ears on the ring, start the sleeve onto the speed gear cone"
The sleeve does not engage the cone. The cone is only the friction/braking surface. The sleeve eventually engages the small clutch/dog teeth on the side of the speed gear.
Your description that the blocker ring "blocks a bad shift" is especially accurate. That's essentially why it is called a blocker ring. It doesn't just synchronize speeds; its slight rotational movement prevents the sleeve teeth from going through until the speed difference has been reduced enough.
Also:
> "the ring ... can move a little on the speed gear"
Close, but think of it as the blocker ring having limited rotational freedom relative to the hub. When the ring contacts the spinning gear cone, friction twists the ring slightly. That twist puts the ring's teeth out of alignment with the sleeve. Once the relative-speed difference disappears, the frictional twisting force disappears and the sleeve can move through.
And your keys idea is basically right. The synchronizer keys/struts fit into slots in the hub underneath the sleeve. Springs preload the keys. As the sleeve begins moving, the keys transfer that movement to the blocker ring so the blocker ring contacts the gear cone before the sleeve can reach the gear's engagement teeth.
One terminology correction: I wouldn't say "sleeve and ring is a whole synchronizer assembly." The complete synchronizer assembly generally means the hub, sliding sleeve, blocker/synchronizer rings, keys/struts/inserts, and springs. The speed gear and shift fork are associated with it but aren't normally considered part of the synchronizer assembly itself.
For ASE A3, the important mental picture is:
**Hub = permanently tied to shaft.
Gear = freely spinning until selected.
Sleeve = locks gear to hub.
Blocker ring = uses friction to match speed and physically prevents premature engagement.
Keys/struts = make the blocker ring hit the cone before the sleeve reaches the gear teeth.**
And one subtle point worth remembering: the synchronizer isn't really "synchronizing the sleeve to the gear." The sleeve/hub are already rotating at shaft speed. It is using friction to bring the freewheeling speed gear to approximately the same speed as the hub/sleeve/shaft. That's the distinction ASE may exploit in a question."

torque steer:
unequal CV joint angles
unequal length of the shafts
the tendency for the longer drive shaft to twist

torque steer is probably going to be on the ase. it just seems like that type of topic.

torque steer most common cause, is unequal cv joint angles.
wobble socket, on extension, if its perfectly straight, it still works, but its not as much torque, u eventually get to a angle where u lose torque, and the wobby whips around and dont work.
imagine we had bad engine or transmission mounts, causeing cv angles to be unequal, as long as they are equal, torque steer is not a issue.

similarity: torque steer in transmissions and drivtrain, and bump steer in suspension and steering... interesting crossover.

in transaxle transmissions, the input shaft is also called the clutch shaft, and the main shaft is sometimes called the countershaft,
this is because the input shaft is not seperate from the mainshaft, input shaft turns the mainshaft, the what would otherwise be a countershaft, becomes the output shaft.

we dont need a pilot bearing on a transaxle because the input shaft is part of the mainshaft, and its one assembly. it dont need a pilot bearing, because the mainshaft assembly is fully supported by the bearings.

on a longitudinal transmission/ ie non transaxle, the input shaft is seperate from the mainshaft, and therefore needs a 3rd bearing to support it.
